The Hässle codes

This site follows development codes, the names a drug carries before it gets an INN: collecting them from named sources, checking each against the record, counting the trials they bring back, and publishing them to Wikidata with references. The first collections were recent drugs, the FDA novel approvals of 2025 and top sellers. Their codes are in ClinicalTrials.gov, with a sponsor (field notes 1–3). This note goes back in time, to the codes of one Swedish company.

When I met Chris Southan recently, he asked if I used MeSH. I did not. He said in passing that it could be useful for old codes. That sent me looking.

At AstraZeneca I worked on a product list for clinical trials. Now semi-retired, I am still fascinated by the topic, so the old codes I thought of were the H numbers from Hässle in Mölndal, outside Göteborg. Most of all Losec. Before omeprazole had a name it was H 168/68. As I remember it, the number was taken from the page numbers in the lab notebook. That is a memory, not a finding, and this note is not about it.

It is about the codes of older compounds. Metoprolol was H 93/26, felodipine H 154/82, ximelagatran H 376/95. Most Hässle codes are older than the trial registries. So the question changed: what can the public record say about a code from 1968?

What was done

I collected H codes from MeSH, ChEMBL, GSRS, NCI Thesaurus, PubMed and Wikidata (Method). MeSH, ChEMBL and GSRS were not in the harvester before (learning 2). I also used AB Hässle's US patents, and the published history of the omeprazole programme (Olbe, Carlsson and Lindberg 2003; Sjöstrand, Olbe and Fellenius 1999).

The collection gives two outcomes, not one:

  • A large historical list: every H code found, 138 codes, each with its sources and its earliest date.
  • A small Wikidata list: only the codes of substances with an INN, 18 codes. 17 of them are now on Wikidata as development code statements (field note 3). Only 3 say who conferred the code. More on that below.

I did not count trials. For a historical collection the archaeology is the product, not the trial count.

What I learned

1. Looking across sources finds odd things, most of all for older compounds. Each source froze a different moment. GSRS keeps the salt. MeSH keeps an entry term from the 1980s. A patent has the dihydrochloride as first isolated. A company register has a study code from 1998. Side by side, the gaps show.

2. The old codes changed the harvester. I first harvested the H codes in a separate notebook. It showed what the shared harvester lacked. Chris was right: MeSH carries most of the H codes, 43 of the 53 harvested codes have a MeSH record. But MeSH was not a source. And many codes sit only on a salt record, such as H 168/68 SODIUM, or esomeprazole's H 199/18, found only on its sodium and magnesium salts. The same happens with recent drugs: GSK573719A sits on umeclidinium bromide. So the shared harvester got three more sources: GSRS, ChEMBL and MeSH. Each is reached from the substance's UNII and followed to its salt records. Codes written with a slash now count as codes. It is still one notebook, and it grows with each collection. Run again on the two earlier collections, it changed no accepted code, but it brought new candidates to review.

3. The rules changed too. The most important new rule: a separator inside the number is part of the code. Written without it, "H 234/09" becomes "H-23409", and in ClinicalTrials.gov that is a Baylor IRB number (NCT00831480), not almokalant. Recent codes such as BMS-562247-01 keep their separator in the trial records too, so the rule now holds for all collections.

4. "Linked" is not "names the code". The harvest listed what the sources link to the substance through the UNII: parents, moieties, class concepts. NCIt C29576 came up on three different beta blockers. Only three NCIt concepts in the INN list actually carry an H code as a synonym (C716, C72727, C87630). A reference must be a record that names the code. The rest is context.

5. A review must not feed the evidence it reviews. I found two circular paths. First, the review rationale was used as a basis for Hässle status. Later, the word "AstraZeneca" in one rationale silently changed the status of esomeprazole's H 199/18. Both are removed. The rule now: status reads evidence files only.

6. Who used a code is not who conferred it. Hässle, Astra Hässle and AstraZeneca R&D Mölndal is one line: the same site and the same numbering. The highest code found is H 510/45. So an AstraZeneca Mölndal affiliation counts. But H codes also appear with Astra Läkemedel and Astra Alab in Södertälje (zimelidine H 102/09, H 75/12, H 77/77). "Astra" alone does not mean Hässle. The site matters.

7. Old codes have few affiliations. Only 19 of the 53 harvested codes have any PubMed record with an affiliation. Records from the 1960s and 70s mostly have none. For the oldest codes, attribution rests on the published history or on patents. Omeprazole is the clearest case. Four PubMed records name H 168/68, from 1983, 1984, 1986 and 2015. The first three have no affiliation. The 2015 review, from Karolinska, says that H168/68 was synthesized in 1979 and given the name omeprazole, but it does not name Hässle.

8. Patents carry most of the early record, and I chose not to count them. 81 codes are known only from patents: two Hässle patent tables of 1968 and 1969 (Brändström and Carlsson), and the zimelidine patents of 1971. Patents are a new domain for me. I keep them visible, with dates and structures, but no conclusion rests on a patent alone. The effect is large. With patents, felodipine's earliest date is 1978. Without them it is 2006.

9. A company's own register reaches further back than the shared registries. astrazenecaclinicaltrials.com keeps studies from before the US registration law. SH-QBE-0020, started in 1998, names H 199/18 together with omeprazole, and it never reached ClinicalTrials.gov.

10. A harvest misses what it does not spell. Melagatran's H 319/68 was missed. The only candidate was the Wikidata alias "H-319-68", and PubMed reads that as "319 68". Searched as "H 319/68", PubMed has two records from 2002, one with an AstraZeneca R&D Mölndal affiliation. That made H 319/68 established. And a code no source offers is never a candidate at all. The same 2015 review names H77/67, a step on the way from the first compound, CMN 131, to the benzimidazoles. It is the only PubMed record with that code. It has no INN, no patent in my set and no PubChem entry. I added it to the large list by hand.

11. Some decisions are per trial, not per code. Omeprazole is a probe drug in an NCI trial (NCT01218620). I skipped it in review, but the harvest still counts it, because the review can only exclude a code. The harvest now has an exclusion per trial.

What went to Wikidata

On 22 November I sent 17 development code statements to Wikidata (property P15001), one per code. Tazolol, H 110/38, stays out: one MeSH line, and the originator code is not settled.

For recent drugs the reference was a ClinicalTrials.gov record, with the company as lead sponsor. For Hässle codes that does not work. So the references are:

  • a PubMed record, but only if its title or abstract contains the code. I checked every one against the record text. A search hit is not enough;
  • MeSH or NCI Thesaurus only where no PubMed record names the code;
  • no aggregated synonym lists. Felodipine's earliest PubMed record is a LactMed book chapter, where H 154/82 sits in a list of synonyms. I used MeSH instead.

14 statements have a PubMed reference, 2 MeSH and 1 NCI Thesaurus.

Only 3 of the 17 say "conferred by Hässle AB". These are almokalant H 234/09, melagatran H 319/68 and ximelagatran H 376/95. Their PubMed records have an Astra Hässle or AstraZeneca R&D Mölndal affiliation. For the other 14 I could not show the company from a record anyone can check. That includes omeprazole, H 168/68. For it, and for H 83/69 and H 149/94, the link between code and company is in the published history (Olbe 2003). But there it sits in a figure behind a paywall, and the abstract names no code. So in the large list these codes are now "established (published history, not re-checked)", together with two more codes from the same kind of source. The list and Wikidata say the same thing.

This is a new bar. All earlier statements from this work have a company. Here I publish the code and leave the company out where it is not shown. The statement still says what is shown: this is a development code of this substance, and here is a record that names it. The company can be added later, if the evidence turns up.

The code of Losec, the best-known Hässle product, cannot be tied to Hässle from open records without patents. I did not expect that. It is the next thing to dig into.

The numbers themselves

How the H numbers were built is not settled here. My memory of the lab notebook is one reading. The public record cannot confirm it, and I set the question aside to follow the codes instead. I may go further on this later, for example in Hässle's archive at Riksarkivet in Göteborg (Hässle AB:s, Mölndal, arkiv, GLA/C0126).

If you worked at Hässle, or know how the codes were given, I would very much like to hear from you.

The lists and their inputs are in the repository: collections/hassle_h_codes_all.csv, collections/hassle_h_codes_wikidata.csv and hassle_codes/inputs/, with a source for every value.
